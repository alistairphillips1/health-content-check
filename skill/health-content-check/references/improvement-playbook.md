# Improvement playbook

How to fix what the gate finds. Each entry: the pattern, why it matters to the reader and the fix as publishable text.

Use the author's own subject matter in every rewrite. The examples below are illustrative shapes, not text to paste.

## 1. The unsourced number
**Pattern:** "Around 90% of patients are back to normal within six weeks."
**Why it matters:** the reader plans their life around it. Unsourced, it is the author's impression, and impressions run optimistic.
**Fix, in order of preference:** find the source and cite it; replace with a sourced figure even if less flattering; or replace with an honest range and say where it comes from. As a last resort, remove the number and describe the shape: "Most people are back to light activity within a few weeks, but recovery varies a lot and some people take several months."
**Never:** substitute a plausible figure. This is the failure that does real harm.

## 2. The overclaim
**Pattern:** "This treatment cures the condition." "Proven to work."
**Fix:** match the verb to the evidence. "Cures" needs curative evidence. "Helps most people" needs an outcome study with numbers. Where evidence is mixed, say so: "Studies disagree about how well this works. The largest trial found [X], a more recent one found [Y]."
**Test:** could the strongest sentence on the page be defended, out loud, to someone who read the underlying papers?

## 3. The benefits-only page
**Pattern:** three paragraphs on how well it works, one line saying "as with any procedure there are risks".
**Why it matters:** this is the pattern that gets healthcare websites into trouble, and it is the reason a reader cannot trust provider-written information by default.
**Fix:** give risks their own heading, list the material ones with frequencies and include what happens if it does not work. Material means what this reader would want to know, including rare things that are serious.

## 4. The wall of clinical prose
**Pattern:** correct, complete, unreadable.
**Fix:** one idea per paragraph, headings a reader could scan in ten seconds and a short "what this page covers" at the top. Convert any sentence with two commas and a subclause into two sentences. Cut every word that survives deletion.
**Before:** "Following the administration of local anaesthetic, which is typically well tolerated, the procedure itself, which takes approximately twenty minutes, is undertaken."
**After:** "You will have a local anaesthetic injection. The procedure takes about 20 minutes."

## 5. Percentages nobody can picture
**Pattern:** "0.5% risk of infection."
**Fix:** natural frequencies with a consistent denominator. "About 1 in 200 people get an infection." Keep the same denominator across the page so risks can be compared. Give the base rate as well as the change when describing a risk factor, because "doubles your risk" is meaningless alone.

## 6. The missing "what if I do nothing"
**Pattern:** the page presents the intervention as the only path.
**Fix:** a short section on doing nothing and on the conservative options, with the same honesty as the main option. Many conditions improve without treatment. Saying so is the clearest signal of trustworthiness a provider page can send.

## 7. The undated page
**Fix:** author, role, date written and date of next review, visible to the reader, not in a footer nobody reads. Then diarise the review with a named owner. If a review date has already passed, that is a Required fix, because the page currently tells the reader something untrue.

## 8. The testimonial standing in for evidence
**Pattern:** "Sarah, 54, was back playing tennis in three weeks."
**Fix:** patient stories are legitimate and valuable, but they are experience, not evidence, and they must be labelled as one person's experience, kept away from the outcome claims and used only with documented written consent. If a story is doing the work a figure should do, replace it with the figure and keep the story for what it is good at, which is telling the reader what it felt like.

## 9. The buried lede
**Pattern:** history, anatomy and mechanism first. What the reader must decide, last.
**Fix:** invert. What is it, what are my options, what happens next, then the background for whoever wants it. Assume the reader is anxious, on a phone, reading the first screen only.

## 10. The frightening sentence
**Pattern:** an accurate risk stated in a way that terrifies without informing.
**Fix:** keep the fact, add the frame. Give the frequency, say what is done to reduce it and say what the reader should watch for and who to call. Never soften by deletion. Anxiety usually comes from not knowing what to do, not from knowing the risk.

## 11. The orphan page with no way out
**Fix:** every page ends with what to do next, who to contact and where to get help urgently if things go wrong. Link the national source on the topic even where it is a competitor's or the NHS's. Withholding a better source to keep the reader on your site is the clearest failure of the reader's interest on this list.

## 12. Priority order when everything is wrong
Fix in this order. Stop and hand back after the first three if time is short.
1. Anything that could lead a reader to a harmful decision: wrong number, overclaim, missing serious risk, no route to urgent help.
2. Gate 0 failures, that is marketing dressed as information.
3. Missing author, date or review date.
4. Readability.
5. Everything else.

---

## Rewrite discipline
- Give the fix as text the author can paste. A note saying "consider simplifying" achieves nothing.
- Change the fewest words that fix the problem. It is their voice.
- Never introduce a fact, a number or a claim in a rewrite that was not in the original and sourced. Rewriting is not the moment to add content.
- If a fix requires clinical knowledge you should not supply, say what is missing and let the author write it.
